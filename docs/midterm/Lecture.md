#### Sex and Gender: First Midterm Question

> "This is the very first question in your midterm exam."

- The professor said the first midterm question asks students to **define the differences between sex and gender**.

##### Sex

- **Sex** refers to biological features of the body.

- It describes biological facts that people are born with.

- Sex focuses on three main areas:

  - **Chromosomes**

    - Common examples: **XX** and **XY**.

  - **Hormones**

    - Examples include **progesterone** and **testosterone**.

    - Everyone has multiple hormones, but the levels usually differ.

  - **Physical characteristics of reproductive organs**

    - Examples: **uterus**, **vagina**, **penis**.

    - Sex also relates to reproductive capacity, such as pregnancy and giving birth.

- Sex provides facts that can be **proven**, **tested**, or biologically observed.

- The professor said sex can be changed in some ways, but its **nature** cannot be fully changed.

  - A person may change the visible characteristics of reproductive organs through surgery.

  - A person may change hormone levels through injections.

  - But the professor stressed that these changes do not fully change the biological nature a person was born with.

  - Example: if hormone injections stop, the body may return toward its original hormone pattern.

##### Gender

- **Gender** is **made**, not natural.

- Gender is a **social construct**.

- Gender includes:

  - **Gender roles**

  - Behaviors

  - Personality

  - Character

  - Social expectations

  - Roles in family and society

- Gender explains how society expects people to behave as men, women, or other identities.

- Unlike sex, gender is not simply a biological fact.

- Gender is shaped by social learning, culture, norms, and repeated behavior.

#### Gender as a Binary Concept

> "Gender is part of binary concept."

- The professor told students to highlight this point.

- **Binary concept** means dividing gender into two fixed categories:

  - **Man / woman**

  - **Male / female**

- The binary concept influences:

  - **Social norms**

  - **Culture**

  - **Gender roles**

  - **Gender expectations**

- Binary thinking tells society:

  - What men should do.

  - What women should do.

  - What qualities men and women should have.

  - What roles men and women should perform for family and society.

##### Binary Logic and "Nature"

- In binary thinking, gender is treated as **natural**, not chosen.

- Binary logic assumes:

  - Gender is something people are born with.

  - Gender cannot or should not change.

  - Behaviors, roles, and identities are determined by nature.

- Example of binary assumptions about women:

  - Women are naturally emotional and sensitive.

  - Therefore, women should not hold leadership roles or make major decisions.

- Example of binary assumptions about men:

  - Men are naturally strong, tough, rational, and emotionally controlled.

  - Therefore, men should be leaders and decision-makers.

- The professor stressed that this logic **limits both men and women**.

  - Not all men want leadership roles.

  - Not all women are weak or emotional.

  - Binary expectations reduce personal freedom and life opportunities.

- A student asked whether binary thinking is similar to a sexualized stereotype.

  - The professor answered that it is a **conservative idea** that sees only male/female or man/woman.

#### Before 1950: Sex and Gender Used Interchangeably

- Before around **1950**, people in English-language contexts often used **sex** and **gender** interchangeably.

- Many people saw the two words as basically the same.

- Around the 1950s, scholars began making a clearer distinction between them.

- The first groups mentioned were:

  - **Psychologists**

  - **Social scientists**

#### Psychologists: Gender as Chosen and Developed

- Psychologists argued that **gender is something people choose to be**.

- People can develop their own **gender identity**.

- From this view:

  - Gender is **not natural**.

  - Gender is **made**.

  - Gender is shaped through personal development and social limits.

- However, psychologists also noticed that people's choices were historically limited.

  - In conservative societies, people often had only two visible choices: man or woman.

  - People usually copied the role models closest to them.

  - A daughter might copy the mother's role.

  - A son might copy the father's role.

- The professor connected this to earlier societies where diversity was not openly accepted.

  - People had fewer role models.

  - People had fewer public examples of different gender identities.

  - People often chose from what they could see around them.

#### Social Order in Earlier Societies

- The professor explained why earlier societies valued strict social order.

- Earlier societies needed **stability**.

- Social order limited freedom, but it made society more predictable and organized.

- The professor's logic:

  - Too many choices can slow decision-making.

  - If people spend too much time exploring identities or careers, society may lose productivity.

  - Fewer choices can keep people focused on assigned duties.

  - This helps society develop roads, hospitals, water supply, electricity, and other basic needs.

- The professor was not saying social order is still necessary in the same way.

- In modern societies:

  - Basic systems are more established.

  - People have more knowledge and global awareness.

  - People demand more freedom and diversity.

  - Modern development depends more on creativity, challenge, and exploration.

#### Social Scientists: Gender Roles Are Learned

- Social scientists explained that **gender roles are behaviors**.

- Gender roles are learned from:

  - **Parents**

  - **Social norms**

  - **Cultural expectations**

- This is a **nurture** explanation.

- Gender is learned through family, society, and culture rather than determined only by biology.

#### Four Important Theories for Gender Study

> "I may not ask you directly about these four theories. But if you can apply some of your answers, I will give you more credits."

- The professor said these theories are useful for improving midterm answers.

#### Biological Essentialism

- **Biological essentialism** argues that males and females have **fixed natural traits**.

- It is friendly to the binary concept because it supports the idea that men and women are naturally different.

- It explains gender differences through:

  - Chromosomes

  - Hormones

  - Reproductive organs

- Main claims:

  - Men are naturally more **rational** and **assertive**.

  - Women are naturally more **emotional** and **nurturing**.

  - Nature shapes people's roles and behaviors.

  - Humans do not freely choose their roles; nature assigns them.

- The professor described this theory as extreme because it places **nature above human freedom and choice**.

- Useful applications:

  - **Medical research**

  - Studying different biological needs and treatments for male and female bodies.

  - Evolutionary explanations.

- Limitation from the slide:

  - It can **oversimplify** gender and sex differences.

#### Social Constructionism

- **Social constructionism** argues that gender is **not fixed**.

- People learn and create genders through society.

- Gender is shaped by:

  - Family members

  - Social norms

  - Culture

  - Everyday interactions

  - Institutions

  - Expectations

- Social constructionism strongly separates **sex** and **gender**.

  - Sex and gender are different things.

  - Biological essentialism tends to connect them.

  - Social constructionism treats gender as social, not biological.

- Social constructionists ask questions such as:

  - What makes men behave in certain ways?

  - What shapes clothing expectations for men and women?

  - What choices does each gender receive?

  - How do norms influence individual identity?

- This theory is useful for analyzing:

  - Norms

  - Identity

  - Institutions

  - Everyday behavior

#### Queer Theory

- **Queer theory** became significant around the **1970s onward**.

- It shows that society was moving toward more awareness of diversity.

- Queer theory challenges fixed categories of gender and sexuality.

- It argues that identities can be **fluid** and unstable rather than fixed forever.

- The lecture emphasized that queer theory focuses mostly on **gender identity**.

- The professor distinguished **gender identity** from **sexuality**:

  - Gender identity describes how a person understands themselves.

  - Sexuality describes attraction, desire, and romantic or sexual feeling.

- Example:

  - A person may identify as a man, but that does not guarantee attraction to women.

  - A person may identify as a woman and still feel attraction to transgender or non-binary people.

- The professor said social scientists view sexuality as a **choice** or as based on personal feeling, not fate.

- The professor contrasted this with a more biological/scientific view:

  - Some scientists connect sex and gender.

  - Some scientists defend binary systems because they worry that too much sexual freedom could reduce reproduction and threaten human survival.

- The slide adds that queer theory is useful for studying:

  - LGBTQ+ identities

  - Norms

  - Resistance

  - How identities are performed and regulated

#### Intersectionality

- **Intersectionality** argues that gender studies should not focus only on gender.

- Gender should be studied together with other social issues, including:

  - **Race**

  - **Class**

  - **Sexuality**

  - **Disability**

  - Other forms of discrimination

- Example from the lecture:

  - An African American woman may experience discrimination as a woman and because of race.

  - If she is disabled, she may also experience discrimination based on her body.

  - If she is poor, class discrimination may also affect her.

- The main point:

  - Different inequalities combine to create unique experiences.

  - You cannot study gender in isolation.

#### John Money and the 1950s Development of "Gender"

- The lecture and slide identify **John Money** as the person who brought the word **gender** back into importance around the **1950s**.

- Before this, sex and gender were often treated as the same.

- John Money described gender as the **roles people have in society**.

- He connected gender roles with:

  - **Femininity**

  - **Masculinity**

- The professor said John Money was the first to point out that gender is a **social construct**.

- In the slide wording, gender was developed in 1950 and John Money is listed as the founder of the word.

#### Simone de Beauvoir

- The lecture identifies Simone de Beauvoir as one of the scholars who extended the meaning of gender.

- The professor summarized her idea as:

  - Humans are born to **become** something.

  - People are not simply born into a finished social role.

  - People make choices about what they become.

- The key implication:

  - People should not be assigned their identity only by society, religion, or parents.

  - Gender is connected to becoming, choice, and social development.

#### Judith Butler

- Judith Butler explained gender as a **tool used to perform what we are**.

- Gender is something people **perform repeatedly**.

- The gender people choose becomes real through repeated action.

- Example from the lecture:

  - A person born with female reproductive organs may prefer masculine qualities.

  - If that person repeatedly performs masculine behavior, they may understand themselves as a man.

- Main point:

  - Gender is not only something a person "has."

  - Gender is something a person repeatedly **does**.

#### Past and Modern Views of Gender

- In the past:

  - People often treated sex and gender as the same.

  - Men and women were expected to follow strict roles.

  - Work, family life, clothing, and behavior were shaped by binary expectations.

  - These roles were often treated as natural even though they were shaped by culture.

- In the modern day:

  - Gender is understood as something people can choose and develop.

  - People can adopt different combinations of masculine and feminine qualities.

  - Gender is widely understood in scholarship as:

    - **Socially constructed**

    - **Dynamic**

    - **Diverse**

#### Not All Societies Follow Strict Binary Gender

- The professor said not all societies strictly value binary gender.

- Some societies recognize:

  - More than two gender identities.

  - Flexible expressions of identity.

  - Greater public space for non-binary and queer people.

- Thailand was used as an example.

  - The professor connected Thailand's relative gender diversity to more flexible religion and culture.

  - Buddhism and Thai culture were described as relatively lenient and adaptive compared with stricter religious or cultural systems.

- The slide emphasizes that gender has always had **cultural variation**, even if Western perspectives often emphasized binary systems.

##### 2. Recognize Diversity in Identity

- Understanding gender helps people recognize that there are more identities than binary man/woman.

- Recognition makes proper respect possible.

  - Misunderstanding can happen.

#### Why Gender Binary Became the Norm

> "Your next question. Why was the gender binary the norm?"

- The professor signaled this as a likely exam question.

- The question asks why many people saw binary gender as common, natural, or normal.

##### 1. Lack of Public Space for Queer and Non-Binary People

- Queer, LGBTIQ+, third-gender, and non-binary people often had no public space to express themselves.

- They had to hide their identities or express them only in private.

- Because society did not see them publicly, society assumed only binary identities existed.

- If people could express their identities in public, others would learn that more identities exist.

##### 2. Lack of Role Models

- Queer and non-binary people often lacked examples to follow.

- A child who felt "born in the wrong body" might only see a father performing masculinity and a mother performing femininity.

- Without visible role models, the child might not know how to understand or express their identity.

- To be accepted as normal, people often covered their true gender identity and followed binary expectations.

##### 3. Lack of Self-Understanding and Information

- Some queer and non-binary people did not fully understand their own feelings.

- They were still exploring who they were.

- Because there were few records, examples, or labels, they did not know where they fit.

- Limited information about alternative gender identities kept people from coming out.

##### 4. Major Institutions Reinforced Binary Gender

- Many major institutions were built around binary categories.

- Examples:

  - **Law**

  - **Religion**

  - **Family**

  - **Schools**

  - Government records and social systems

- In the past, laws often protected only men and women, not third-gender or non-binary people.

- Religion often assigned roles to men and women but not queer people.

- Families used father/mother roles as the basic model.

- Schools often required students to choose either male uniforms or female uniforms.

- Because institutions recognized only two genders, it was difficult for non-binary people to receive justice, recognition, or equal treatment.

##### 5. Limits of Language

- In the past, people lacked words to describe many gender identities.

- The professor mentioned labels such as:

  - **Transgender**

  - **Cisgender**

  - **Cishet**

- Without labels and clear definitions, people found it difficult to identify themselves.

- Without language, society could not easily recognize or discuss gender diversity.

##### 6. Social Pressure

- People hid their identities because they feared punishment for being seen as strange or different.

- They feared:

  - Exclusion

  - Loss of social belonging

  - Loss of status

  - Loss of jobs or leadership positions

- In the past, some people viewed LGBTQ+ people as mentally sick.

- If someone in a high-status position was known as non-binary, society might question their ability to make decisions.

- The more people hid, the less society learned about gender diversity.

##### 7. Institutional Reinforcement

- The professor closed with the point that institutions reinforced binary gender.

- This overlaps with earlier points about law, religion, family, and school.

- The slide adds that schools, laws, and religious systems repeated the message:

  - There are only two genders.

  - Each gender has a fixed role.

#### Gender Binary, Capitalism, and Patriarchy

- The lecture begins by connecting **gender binary** to **patriarchy** and **capitalism**.

- **Capitalism** depends on production, labor, products, and profit.

  - To produce efficiently, society needs organized labor.

  - **Binary gender roles** assign different work to men and women so the system can run smoothly.

- Under binary thinking:

  - **Men** are viewed as leaders, controllers, active workers, and people with power.

  - **Women** are viewed as subordinate, passive, obedient, and responsible for domestic labor.

- The professor's example:

  - Men work outside the home in industry and production.

  - Women take care of domestic work: cooking, cleaning, ironing, children, and the household.

  - Because women do unpaid or underpaid domestic labor, men can rest at home and return to work with more energy.

  - This division of labor supports economic productivity under capitalism.

- Binary also supports **patriarchy**.

  - Men hold primary power in politics, economics, religion, property, and social authority.

  - Binary helps men keep that power because women are taught not to question or challenge male authority.

  - A society with less questioning appears more peaceful and predictable, but this "order" is built on inequality.

> "Gender was not just identity, but a way to maintain power, social order, and hierarchy."

- This is an important exam-relevant claim.

  - Gender is not only about how someone identifies.

  - Gender can be used as a system of control that places people into unequal social positions.

#### Patriarchal Systems and Property

- In a **patriarchal system**, men control primary resources and institutions.

  - Political leadership

  - Economic power

  - Property ownership

  - Religious or spiritual authority

- When men own the property and resources, women become more vulnerable.

  - Women may become dependent on men for survival.

  - The more women depend on men, the more society treats women as men's property or objects.

  - Women become "speechless" in the sense that they have little power to speak, decide, or act independently.

- This connects directly to the study guide theme of **patriarchy** and gender inequality.

#### Binary as Social Order

- **Gender binary** helps make society more **predictable** and **controllable**.

- If society can control people's behavior, it can predict future outcomes.

  - If men are trained to be productive, active, and hardworking, society expects high output and economic growth.

  - If women are trained to be quiet and not challenge authority, society expects less conflict.

- Binary creates:

  - **Clear division of labor**

  - **Defined family structures**

  - **Stable expectations**

  - Clear ideas of what fathers, mothers, and children should do

- The professor notes that this order can make relationships and society seem smoother, but it also limits freedom and reinforces inequality.

#### Religious Influence

- Religion is one of the institutions that reinforces gender binary.

- In many religious traditions, gender is treated as something **given by God** or a higher power.

  - If a person is born with male reproductive organs and identifies as a man, religion may frame that as divinely chosen.

  - Gender is treated as natural or sacred rather than socially constructed.

- Religion often assigns different roles to men and women.

  - In the professor's Christianity example, a "good Christian woman" is expected to be a mother, care for children, care for her husband, and care for the house.

  - In the Buddhism example, a man may prove manhood by ordaining as a monk at least once.

- Important point: religion can connect gender to **right and wrong behavior**.

  - Example: a man wearing a skirt may be judged as "wrong," not just "inappropriate."

  - A woman wearing a skirt may be judged as "right."

- This limits **freedom of expression** because clothing, appearance, and behavior become moral judgments.

- The professor connects this to the assignment example where a man wears a tight skirt and high heels while still identifying as a man.

  - Clothing can be fashion or expression.

  - Society often misreads it as proof of gender or sexuality.

#### Gender Roles

- **Gender roles** are **socially constructed** expectations.

  - They are not natural facts.

  - Society creates them and then endorses or enforces them.

- Gender roles influence:

  - **Behavior**

  - **Appearance**

  - **Interests**

  - **Careers**

- Example:

  - If men are expected to work outside the home and lead, they must appear strong and behave strongly.

  - If women are expected to do domestic labor, they are trained toward caregiving, softness, and home-based responsibilities.

#### How People Learn Gender Roles

- People learn gender roles from many institutions:

  - **Family**: parents teach ideas about masculinity, femininity, duties, and behavior.

  - **Media**: films, dramas, actors, and characters model gender performances.

  - **Education**: uniforms, subjects, rules, and school expectations divide students by gender.

  - **Culture and religion**: traditions define what men and women should do.

  - **Government**: laws and policies can treat men and women differently.

  - **Workplaces**: job assignments, leave policies, and pay structures can reinforce gender roles.

- The professor emphasizes that education can both open and limit the mind.

  - In the past, girls were encouraged to take home economics: cooking, sewing, cleaning.

  - Boys were encouraged toward engineering, agriculture, production, and manufacturing.

  - This is gender bias inside schools.

#### Workplace Policies and Gender Roles

- Workplaces can enforce gender roles through leave policies and job assignments.

- Maternity leave example:

  - In the past, women could take time off for childbirth and child care.

  - Some companies paid full salary, some cut salary, and some gave unpaid leave while holding the job.

  - The professor argues that children should be both mother's and father's responsibility.

  - Paternity leave is a better modern policy because both parents care for children.

- Dangerous-job example:

  - Some factories reserve dangerous or physically difficult jobs for men.

  - These jobs may pay more, so men gain access to higher wages.

  - Women may be assigned "soft" or "mild" tasks such as accounting, inventory, or marketing.

  - This creates workplace discrimination and unequal income.

#### Why Gender Roles Are Important

> "There will be one or two questions associated with gender roles."

- Gender roles matter because they **shape gender identity**.

  - They help people understand who they are and how they are expected to behave.

- Gender roles can organize relationships.

  - In traditional relationships, duties are fixed, so there may be less negotiation.

  - In modern relationships, couples often compromise, such as one person cooking and the other cleaning.

  - Negotiation can be fairer, but it can also create conflict if partners cannot agree.

- Gender roles influence:

  - **Career paths**

  - **Education**

  - **Access to leadership**

- Example:

  - A wife who must do domestic labor and care for children may have less time for higher education, career goals, or leadership.

- Gender roles also define what society sees as **acceptable** and **not acceptable**.

  - This limits diversity because people are pushed toward one standard.

  - It limits self-expression because people fear being judged.

- Gender roles reinforce **inequality** and create **stereotypes**.

  - Example: men are expected to be strong leaders and may be told not to cry in public.

  - These stereotypes can be toxic for men as well as women.

#### Gender Identity

- **Gender identity** means how a person understands and describes themself.

- The professor distinguishes two types:

  - **False gender identity**

  - **True gender identity**

- **False gender identity** happens when someone lives in a conservative society that accepts only binary gender.

  - If they reveal their real identity, they may be judged, condemned, excluded, or punished.

  - They fake or force an identity so they can blend in.

- **True gender identity** is possible in a more liberal society.

  - People can express who they are with less fear.

  - Society appreciates difference and allows freer self-expression.

#### Gender Identity and Development

- The professor connects gender identity to national and workplace development.

- If people must hide their gender identity:

  - They spend energy deciding how to dress, move, speak, and act so others will not notice a mismatch between sex and gender.

  - They lose time and mental energy before even arriving at work.

  - They may contribute less creativity, innovation, and productivity.

- This becomes a waste of **human resources** and **human potential**.

- If people can express gender identity freely:

  - They carry less stress and burden.

  - They can focus more energy on work, creativity, and social contribution.

  - The country can develop better because people are less restricted.

- Gender identity also helps people find where they fit.

  - Someone who is non-binary may choose to live in a country or community where they can be more free.

- Publicly revealing gender identity can teach society about diversity.

  - People learn which pronouns to use.

  - People learn how others want to be treated.

  - Respect is not only a rule taught in school; it is learned through social experience.

- Gender identity supports **inclusivity**, **equality**, and awareness of diversity.

#### Gender Expression

- **Gender expression** is not the same as gender identity, but the two are connected.

- **Gender expression** means the actions, expressions, behaviors, and presentation someone uses to reveal who they are.

- It can include:

  - Clothing

  - Gestures

  - Movement

  - Walking style

  - Communication

  - Word choice

  - Tone and voice

- Two types:

  - **Permanent gender expression**

  - **Temporary gender expression**

- **Permanent gender expression**

  - A person freely and repeatedly expresses their identity.

  - Repeated expression strengthens gender identity.

- **Temporary gender expression**

  - A person must switch expression depending on the setting.

  - Example: acting one way around conservative parents and another way around accepting friends.

  - Constantly switching identity can weaken a person's sense of self and create mental health decline, stress, burden, and fatigue.

- Gender expression is important because it:

#### Liberal Feminism: Rights, Access, and Reform

- **Liberal feminism** argues that women's rights and equality can be gained through **law, policy, education, and institutional reform**.

#### Social Institutions and Patriarchy

- The professor defined **social institutions** as major organized parts of society, such as:

  - **family institutions**

  - **educational institutions**

  - **religious institutions**

  - **governmental institutions**

  - **economic or trade institutions**

  - **health and environmental institutions**

- In earlier society, the **family institution** handled many tasks:

  - Marriage reinforces gender roles.

  - It limits women's freedom.

  - It makes women compromise with husbands and family expectations.

  - It keeps women tied to unpaid domestic duties.

- Gilman argued that women should:

  - Work **outside the home**

  - Receive **paid employment**

  - Gain **education**

  - Build professional careers

  - Become economically independent

- Her reasoning:

  - If women earn their own money, they can take care of themselves.

  - Paid work proves women are socially capable and equal.

  - Economic independence is necessary for liberation.

- Gilman also argued that **housework and childcare keep women dependent and isolated**.

- Her proposed social solutions:

  - **Shared kitchens**

  - **Professional childcare**

  - **Communal domestic services**

- **Communal domestic services** means the community shares domestic labor according to people's skills:

  - A person good at cooking may cook for the community.

  - A person good at cleaning may clean.

  - A person good at sewing may make or repair clothes.

  - Domestic responsibility becomes social and shared, not assigned only to wives and mothers.

- Key point:

  - Gilman did not blame only individual men.

  - She blamed the **social system** that makes women dependent on marriage and domestic work.

- This is why the professor described her as radical:

  - She wanted to reorganize the social system, not only reform one law.

- Slide reference: Gilman's famous work **Women and Economics (1898)** argued that women's liberation required **economic freedom**.

#### Emma Goldman

- **Emma Goldman** was presented as another radical/Marxist feminist thinker.

- She was also described as an **anarchist** because she challenged authority, the state, religion, and social control.

- Goldman's main idea:

  - Voting rights alone cannot liberate women.

  - Women can gain the vote and still remain controlled by family, religion, marriage, and social norms.

- The professor asked whether students agree that gaining the right to vote can liberate women.

- Goldman believed women must liberate themselves by refusing to see themselves as servants.

- She argued women should stop being servants to:

  - The **family**

  - The **husband**

  - The **children**

  - **Society**

  - **Religion**

- Goldman criticized religion because it could control women's bodies and choices:

  - Religion might tell women how many children to have.

  - Religion might restrict abortion or birth control.

  - Goldman argued women should control their own destiny.

- Goldman strongly criticized **marriage**.

- Her critique of marriage:

  - Marriage is a burden to women's success.

  - Marriage turns women into **free labor** for husbands and children.

  - Marriage can make women sexual objects for husbands.

  - Marriage can reduce women's control over their bodies.

  - Marriage can make women appear like property of the husband or his family.

  - Domestic violence continues as long as marriage is treated as an institution of male ownership and control.

- Goldman supported:

  - **Sexual freedom**

  - The right to choose one's partner

  - The right to say no to sex

  - **Birth control**

  - Control over pregnancy

  - Personal independence

- Her solution:

  - Women must **challenge authority**.

  - Women must question power structures in society.

  - Questioning authority creates change.

#### Gilman vs. Goldman

- Both thinkers criticized marriage and women's dependence.

- Both were presented as radical because they challenged the deep structures behind women's oppression.

- Key comparison:

  - **Gilman** focused on **economic independence** and reorganizing domestic labor through community support.

  - **Goldman** focused on **personal freedom**, sexual freedom, birth control, and challenging authority.

- Gilman's solution:

  - Change the social system so domestic work and childcare are shared.

  - Give women paid work and economic independence.

- Goldman's solution:

  - Reject obedience to family, religion, marriage, and state authority.

  - Women must liberate themselves by questioning and challenging power.

- Main difference:

  - **Gilman** wanted a reorganized community system.

  - **Goldman** wanted women to challenge all controlling authorities directly.

### Notes

#### Four Waves and the Problem of Institutions

- Modern Western feminism is often organized into **four waves**:

  - **First wave**: suffrage, property rights, education, citizenship, and marriage/legal reform.

  - **Second wave**: workplace discrimination, reproductive rights, family roles, sexuality, and violence against women.

  - **Third wave**: diversity among women, individuality, race, class, sexuality, and identity.

  - **Fourth wave**: digital activism, sexual harassment, body shaming, rape culture, online misogyny, and gender-based violence.

- The lecture focused first on how feminist organizations behave after a major goal is achieved.

#### Goal-Oriented Institutions

- A **goal-oriented institution** focuses the whole organization on **one main goal**.

- Strengths:

  - Members work in the **same direction**.

  - The organization is easier to unite because everyone agrees on the target.

  - Cooperation is smoother because the institution has a clear priority.

- Weaknesses:

  - Once the goal is achieved, the institution may have **nothing left to do**.

  - If the organization was created only for that single goal, it is supposed to close or transform.

  - The professor warned that institutions often try to continue even after their original purpose is finished.

- The danger is that an institution may become **dishonest to its role** because it wants to keep recognition, trust, money, and power.

- The lecture used political power as an analogy:

  - A leader or group may claim to solve a national crisis.

  - After the crisis is settled, they should return power or step aside.

  - Instead, they may create new reasons to remain in power, becoming oppressive.

- Applied to feminism:

  - Many suffrage groups were organized around the single goal of **women's right to vote**.

  - After suffrage was won, some organizations lost direction or closed.

  - Slides note that after women gained suffrage, feminism in Europe and the United States **lost momentum** because many groups lacked a broader ideology beyond voting rights.

#### Multiple-Goal Institutions

- A **multiple-goal institution** works on several goals at the same time.

- Possible goals in feminist organizations might include:

  - Protecting women from dangerous work.

  - Increasing women's wages.

  - Increasing holidays or leave for women.

  - Supporting education, maternal health, children, poor families, charity, healthcare, or labor protection.

- Strengths:

  - The institution does not become useless after one goal is achieved.

  - It can continue addressing different social problems.

  - It may represent more areas of women's lives than a single-issue movement.

- Weaknesses:

  - Too many goals make it difficult to decide **what should be prioritized**.

  - Labor, money, and attention get divided among many projects.

  - Smaller groups may not have enough workers or financial support to produce quality outcomes.

  - Goals may be achieved more slowly.

  - Conflict can occur because different groups have different working styles, attitudes, and perceptions.

  - Related issues may become separated even when they could cooperate, such as **higher wages for women** and **more holidays for women**.

- The professor described this as **fragmentation**: members work on separate projects instead of helping one another toward a shared direction.

#### Equality, Opportunity, and Protective Laws

> "Which one do you believe is more important?"

- The lecture then moved to an exam-style debate: should feminism prioritize **full equality/opportunity** or **protective laws for women**?

- The slide on **Alice Paul and the Equal Rights Amendment** explains the issue:

  - Alice Paul wanted **full legal equality between men and women**.

  - Some laws protected women workers, such as **maximum eight-hour workdays** and special protections for female factory workers.

  - Supporters argued these laws protected women's health and welfare.

  - Alice Paul argued that these protections could backfire by making women more expensive or less attractive to employers.

- The professor's example:

  - A law might limit women to **eight hours** of work.

  - If employers want women to work longer, they must pay overtime, possibly double or triple the normal rate.

  - Men may be allowed to work longer hours at the normal wage.

  - Employers may then prefer male workers because they can work more hours for lower cost.

- The lecture's cause-effect logic:

  - **Protective laws** can reduce exploitation and dangerous working conditions.

  - But too many protective laws can also limit women's income, training, travel, and self-development.

  - Limited income means fewer life opportunities and less independence.

- This connects directly to the study-guide theme of **equality versus protection**:

  - Equality asks whether women should have the same legal treatment as men.

  - Protection asks whether women need special support because of unequal social conditions.

  - The difficult question is whether protection helps women or restricts them.

#### First-Wave Feminism: Achievements and Limits

- The lecture identified four major first-wave outcomes:

  - **Property rights and economic rights**

  - **Access to education**

  - **Recognition of women as citizens**

  - **Marriage rights and legal reform**

- First-wave feminism was important, but the professor emphasized that it was **not inclusive enough**.

- Its main concerns often reflected **white, middle-class, and upper-class women**.

- Many women were left out:

  - Working-class women

  - Poor women

  - Women from different races

  - Women from different nationalities

  - Women facing multiple forms of inequality at once

- The lecture stressed that their voices and issues were often **not discussed** and **not heard**.

#### Issues First-Wave Feminism Failed to Address

- **Sexuality and reproductive rights**

  - First-wave feminism did not strongly discuss women's freedom in sexuality.

  - It did not fully address women's rights to choose partners, give sexual consent, or make decisions about pregnancy and reproduction.

- **Workplace sexism**

  - First-wave feminism discussed access to work, but not enough about what happened after women entered workplaces.

  - Women faced gendered job segregation, unequal treatment, harassment, and bullying at work.

- **Public life versus private life**

  - First-wave feminism did not clearly explain what should count as **private** and what should count as **public**.

  - The second wave later made this distinction central.

- **Intersectionality**

  - First-wave feminism often failed to understand how **race, class, colonialism, sexuality, and gender** combine.

  - The professor emphasized that women's issues cannot be understood only through gender if racism and class inequality are also shaping women's lives.

#### World War II and Government Propaganda

- The lecture then explained why second-wave feminism emerged from postwar frustration.

- During **World War II**, many men were drafted and sent to different parts of the world.

- This caused a labor shortage in the United States:

  - Industrial production needed workers.

  - Agriculture also needed workers.

  - If the economy declined, the government would have less money to support the military.

- The government had limited options:

  - It did not want to rely heavily on migrants because of wartime fears about spies.

  - Technology could not replace workers quickly enough.

  - The remaining option was to recruit women into the workforce.

- Before the war, government and religious messages had promoted the ideal of the **good American woman**:

  - Stay at home.

  - Be a housewife and mother.

  - Cook, clean, and care for the family.

  - Fit the ideal of the good Christian woman.

- During the war, propaganda changed:

  - Women were encouraged to work outside the home.

  - They were told they could serve the country and become heroines.

  - Work was presented as patriotic and necessary for national survival.

- Many women experienced paid work as a new form of **freedom**, independence, and movement outside the home.

#### Postwar Return to Domesticity

- After the war, the government changed its message again.

- Returning soldiers needed jobs, so women were pushed back into the home.

- Postwar propaganda promoted a return to the **old American life** and the **American Dream**:

  - Marriage

  - Homemaking

  - Motherhood

  - Supporting husbands and children

  - The nuclear family as the ideal life

- Slides describe this as a propaganda war to bring back old American popular culture.

- The professor emphasized that women were manipulated:

  - During the war, they were praised for leaving home to work.

  - After the war, they were told that a perfect woman should return home and make the family perfect.

#### Cars, Appliances, and the Feeling of Freedom at Home

- The lecture connected postwar domesticity to consumer culture.

- Companies such as **Ford** and **General Motors** shifted toward middle-class and working-class consumers by making cars more affordable.

- Access to cars gave some women a feeling of **freedom of movement**:

  - They were less dependent on walking, buses, taxis, or husbands.

  - They felt less trapped at home.

- New household technologies also changed women's domestic experience:

  - Dishwashers

  - Washing machines

  - Dryers

  - Microwaves

  - Electric stoves

- These devices were marketed as giving women control over the house "by pushing a button."

- The professor's point was not that these products solved inequality.

- Instead, they helped make domestic life seem modern, comfortable, and desirable, which supported the postwar image of the happy housewife.

- Entertainment also reinforced this image:

  - Daytime television and series were associated with housewives at home.

  - Popular culture helped normalize the idea that women belonged in the domestic sphere.

#### Second-Wave Feminism

- The **second wave** emerged in the **1960s-1970s** as women questioned the traditional roles promoted after World War II.

- Existing problems included:

  - **Unequal pay**

  - **Legal inequality**

  - **Unequal job opportunities**

  - Lack of childcare services

  - Workplace discrimination

  - Domestic expectations

- The second wave developed more theories and concepts to explain oppression:

  - **Patriarchy**

  - **Gender as social construction**

  - **The personal is political**

  - The role of family in women's oppression

  - Gender inequality in institutions

- The professor contrasted the first wave and second wave:

  - The suffrage movement used many techniques, including radical protest, to win voting rights.

  - The second wave became more connected to education, theory, universities, public debate, and gender studies.

- Second-wave feminism entered academic life through:

  - Women's studies programs

  - Feminist research

  - Gender studies

  - Studies of women's history

  - Studies of representation in literature and media

  - Studies of inequality in institutions

#### Public and Private: "The Personal Is Political"

- A major second-wave idea was that problems treated as **private** are often caused by public power and institutions.

- The study guide highlights **public sphere versus private sphere** as a key concept.

- Examples:

  - Domestic violence is often treated as a family issue, but it is also a political and legal issue.

  - Unequal domestic labor is treated as private household behavior, but it reflects gender roles and social institutions.

  - Workplace discrimination is not just an individual conflict; it reflects institutional inequality.

- The second wave argued that discrimination and injustice cannot be excused just because they happen in the home, family, workplace, or other supposedly private spaces.

### Notes

#### Post-Suffrage Fragmentation

- The lecture opened by continuing the point that, after women won suffrage, many women's organizations became **fragmented**.

- Instead of working together under one broad feminist goal, organizations often focused on **specific issues**:

  - Education.

  - Maternal health.

  - Helping poor families.

  - Improving schools.

- The professor emphasized that these issues were important but **not fully inclusive**:

  - Campaigns for women to enter serious or professional university majors, such as engineering, medicine, and pharmacy, mainly benefited women who already had access to education.

  - **Lower-class women** and **women of color** were often left out because some did not even have access to school.

  - They worked individually.

  - This made them less successful and less socially impactful.

- Modern women's organizations were contrasted as stronger because they often **share resources**, learn from one another, and work together on campaigns.

#### Why the Second Wave Emerged

- The lecture connected the second wave to women becoming tired of being used by the government.

- During wartime, women were encouraged to work and support the country.

- After the war, government and popular culture pushed women back into the home through propaganda about the **American Dream**, marriage, motherhood, homemaking, and supporting husbands.

- The professor framed the second wave as a response to this manipulation:

> Women realized the government used them when needed, then wanted to put them back home.

- This led feminists to create **counter-propaganda** against government propaganda.

#### Feminist Counter-Propaganda

##### "The Personal Is Political"

- The first feminist slogan emphasized in the lecture was **"the personal is political."**

- Meaning:

  - Problems women experience in everyday life are not only personal problems.

  - They are connected to political and social systems, especially **patriarchy**.

- Examples:

  - **Domestic violence**.

  - **Workplace discrimination**.

  - **Unequal labor at home**.

- Feminists argued that patriarchy shaped politics and social institutions, so women's everyday suffering should be treated as a public and political issue.

- Posters and images were used to make the message simple and visible:

  - Women looking exhausted from cooking and cleaning.

  - Images criticizing unequal domestic labor.

##### A New Image of Women

- Feminists also tried to create a **new image of women**.

- Unlike government propaganda such as **Rosie the Riveter**, the second wave did not rely on one fixed figure.

- Instead, feminists promoted ideas:

  - Women should have **confidence in their bodies**.

  - Women should respect their **own abilities**.

  - Women can do anything they choose.

  - Women should not be controlled by beauty standards.

- The professor stressed that the second wave was one of the first movements to openly challenge the **beauty standard**.

- However, the lecture also presented a critical question:

  - The government forced a new image on women.

  - Feminists gave more freedom, but still implied that the old image of women was wrong and needed fixing.

  - This means both sides were, in different ways, trying to reshape women.

> Both sides, the government and the feminists, are trying to enforce new reality of women.

##### Conservative Domestic Image

- The video/example in the lecture showed a conservative image of the "good wife":

  - Make the evening belong to the husband.

  - Do not complain if he comes home late.

  - Do not question his actions, judgment, or integrity.

  - Treat him as the **master of the house**.

  - Listen to him and know one's place.

- This example showed the kind of domestic ideology second-wave feminists were reacting against.

##### "Our Bodies, Ourselves"

- Another slogan was **"Our Bodies, Ourselves."**

- This focused on **self-autonomy** and **self-control**.

- Women should make decisions about:

  - Whether to get pregnant.

  - How many children to have.

  - How to deal with pregnancy and its consequences.

- This connects directly to the study-guide topic of **reproductive rights**.

##### Anti-Beauty-Standard Protest

- The lecture identified the **1968 American beauty pageant protest** as a symbolic anti-beauty-standard event.

- The protest showed that second-wave feminists rejected beauty pageants as institutions that judged and disciplined women's bodies.

##### Artworks and Posters

- Second-wave feminists produced many artworks and posters to raise awareness.

- Examples from the lecture:

  - Women breaking chains.

  - Women throwing away mops, brooms, and domestic tools.

  - Women escaping domestic violence and imagining happier futures.

- These visuals turned feminist arguments into easy public messages.

##### Lesbian Visibility

- The movement eventually promoted **lesbian visibility** and tried to include lesbians under the idea of **sisterhood**.

- However, the professor emphasized that this inclusion was selective:

  - Lesbians became more visible.

  - But mainstream feminists did not fully understand lesbian issues.

  - Lower-class women and women of color were still often excluded.

#### Public vs Private Matters

##### Feminist View

- Feminists tried to separate truly private matters from issues that should be treated as public.

- Examples of private matters:

  - Romantic feelings and relationships.

  - Sexuality as personal choice.

  - Choosing what religion to believe in.

  - Emotions.

  - Choosing whom to marry.

- But the professor stressed that **practice or action** can become public if it harms others:

  - Believing in a religion is private.

  - Practicing a harmful religious act can become public because it affects well-being and community safety.

- For feminists, issues such as **domestic violence** and unequal domestic work are public because they disturb women's well-being.

- In this view, what happens in the family can still become a public matter if it harms someone or damages community security.

##### Conservative View

- Conservative people were described as defining private and public matters differently.

- A matter is private if:

  - It affects only one individual.

  - It happens among members of the same family or group.

  - It takes place at home or on private property.

- A matter is public if:

  - It affects many people.

  - It happens in a public area.

- Conservatives focus strongly on **where the act takes place**:

  - If a crime happens in a private area, they are more likely to classify it as private.

  - If it happens in public and affects many people, it becomes public.

- The professor gave an exam cue to simplify and compare these views.

> When you see the expression in the exam, you should be able to write the answer right away.

#### Tools Used by Second-Wave Feminists

  - Put pressure on government and other institutions.

- **Consciousness-raising groups**

- **Media and popular culture critique**

- **Legal and policy advocacy**

  - Feminists pushed for more laws protecting women.

  - They also wanted legal reform to make the system more fair.

  - The professor emphasized that the second wave produced many laws and reforms compared with later waves.

- **Women's organizations and advocacy groups**

  - Legal organizations gave campaigns legitimacy.

  - Being part of an official institution made it easier to approach government offices and other powerful institutions.

- **Academic institutions**

  - Schools and universities were used to educate people about inequality and discrimination.

- **Workplace equality**

  - Equal pay.

  - Equal opportunity.

  - Access to positions and promotions.

- **Reproductive rights**

  - Access to contraception and birth control.

  - Abortion rights.

  - Maternal health care.

- **Sexual liberation and bodily autonomy**

  - Challenged social expectations that men should desire sex but women should not.

  - Challenged the expectation that sexuality must always be heterosexual.

  - Treated sexuality as a choice and a matter of autonomy.

- **Family and domestic roles**

  - Challenged the conservative idea of **natural roles**.

  - Conservatives claimed women's domestic roles were natural.

  - Feminists argued that calling housework a natural duty creates **free labor** for husbands and families.

- **Violence against women**

  - The slides identify violence against women as a major second-wave focus.

  - This connects to the lecture's discussion of domestic violence as a public and political issue.

- **Education and representation**

#### Minor Feminist Groups in the Second Wave

- The professor noted that many types of feminism appeared during the second wave.

- **Anarcho-feminists**

  - Borrowed from **Emma Goldman**.

  - Suspicious of major institutions such as the family, private property, state power, and religion.

  - Believed women could not be liberated unless they escaped from or dismantled oppressive institutions.

- **Individualist feminists**

  - The lecture/slides described them as turning to government in relation to women's problems.

- **Amazon feminists**

  - Associated liberation with female strength.

- **Separatist feminists**

#### Problems and Limitations of the Second Wave

- **Limited representation**

  - Many campaigns centered on white, middle-class women's issues.

  - Poor women, working-class women, women of color, and women with different social locations were not fully represented.

- **Divisions within the movement**

  - White middle-class women worked on issues such as rights, property, and reproductive rights.

  - Lower-class women felt their voices were not heard.

  - The professor described their question as: are we not women, and are we not human?

- **White feminist perspective**

  - Many white feminists saw **gender discrimination** as the main barrier preventing women from reaching the **American Dream**.

  - They argued that removing discrimination would allow women to access American life: comfort, security, money, infrastructure, leisure, and high living standards.

- **Black feminist critique**

  - Black women argued that gender alone did not explain their problems.

  - Their experiences also involved **race**, **class**, and sometimes **political stance**.

  - This connects to the study-guide topic of **intersectionality**.

- **Exclusion of lesbian women**

  - Lesbians were active but not fully welcomed at first.

  - Mainstream feminists later promoted lesbian visibility partly to reduce criticism.

  - The professor emphasized that visibility did not mean full understanding of lesbian issues.

- **Western bias**

  - Western feminists often saw their movement as the smartest and most legitimate.

  - They distrusted techniques and perspectives used by women in other countries.

  - This connects to later topics on globalization feminism and Global South critiques.

- **Internal debates about sexuality**

  - In this lecture's framing, **liberal feminists** argued that pornography degrades women, turns women into sexual objects, makes women vulnerable, and can contribute to violence.

  - **Radical feminists**, as presented in the lecture, argued that pornography can allow women freedom of expression, self-confidence, income, and control over their own bodies.

  - The professor turned this into an explicit question asking which side is more reasonable.

- **Oversimplifying oppression**

  - Some feminists overemphasized patriarchy, gender discrimination, or institutions as the single root of all problems.

  - The professor criticized this as too narrow because it ignores other factors.

- **Fixed ideas of womanhood**

  - Some second-wave feminists still assumed a fixed idea of what women should be.

  - This excluded women who were masculine, gender nonconforming, or otherwise did not match mainstream expectations of womanhood.

- Conflict between **Western feminists** and women from **less developed countries / the Global South**.

- Debate over **women's rights vs. cultural rights**.

- Debate over whether women's suffering in less developed countries comes mainly from **patriarchy** or **underdevelopment**.

### Notes

#### Western Feminists and Women in Less Developed Countries

- During the **second wave** in the 1960s-1980s, many Western feminists wanted to expand feminist movements into less developed countries.

- British, American, and European feminists joined together to promote feminism internationally.

- They focused especially on **African countries**, with some attention to Asian countries.

- Western feminists tended to view themselves as:

  - **Saviors**.

  - People with a **duty** to raise equality.

  - People who could help end women's suffering around the world.

> Western feminists viewed themselves as "saviors."

- Women in less developed countries often viewed Western feminists very differently:

  - As **outsiders**, not sisters.

  - As people trying to **impose Western ideas**.

  - As people who did not understand local cultures.

  - As people who did not understand the most urgent problems local women faced.

  - As people who could not properly represent or promote Global South women's issues.

- Key criticism:

  - Western women and Global South women often had **different priorities**.

  - Western feminists assumed they knew what problems mattered most.

  - Local women argued that Western feminists ignored economic survival, cultural context, and local needs.

#### Western Priority: Harmful Cultural Practices

- Western feminists often prioritized ending practices such as:

  - **Female infanticide**.

  - **Widow burning (sati)**.

  - **Arranged marriage**.

  - **Female genital cutting (FGC)**.

- Western feminists saw these practices as **violations of women's rights**.

- Some women in less developed countries framed these practices differently:

  - As part of **cultural rights**.

  - As cultural practices they had a right to continue.

  - As traditions outsiders should not simply ban without understanding local society.

- This creates the major exam-relevant conflict:

  - **Women's rights**: protecting women from harmful practices.

  - **Cultural rights**: protecting a community's ability to maintain its traditions.

> "Which one is more important? Women's rights or cultural rights?"

#### 1980 UN Women's Conference and the Patriarchy vs. Underdevelopment Debate

- The slide identifies the case study as the **1980 UN World Conference on Women in Copenhagen**:

  - Full theme: **World Conference of the United Nations Decade for Women: Equality, Development and Peace**.

- After this conference, debates increased over the relationship between Western feminism and women's movements in developing countries.

- Western feminists argued:

  - The root of women's problems in less developed countries was **patriarchy**.

  - To raise equality and protect women's rights, societies needed to reduce or remove patriarchal structures.

- Women in less developed countries argued:

  - The root of their problems was **underdevelopment**.

  - Poverty, weak public services, and unstable economies made women vulnerable.

  - Economic and political conditions had to be fixed before equality could become realistic.

#### Why These Issues Are Urgent

- The slide lists urgent issues:

  - **Poverty**.

  - **Healthcare**.

  - **Education**.

  - **Gender equality**.

  - **Political instability**.

  - **Economic instability**.

  - **Poor infrastructure**.

  - **Overcrowding**.

  - **Environmental problems**.

- Why they are urgent:

#### Cairo Conference, Population Control, and Reproductive Rights

- The slide identifies this case study as the **1994 International Conference on Population and Development in Cairo**.

- The transcript's "1917" appears to be a transcription error because the matching slide is the **1994 Cairo conference**.

- World leaders were concerned about:

  - Rising birth rates.

  - **Overpopulation**.

  - Future shortage of natural resources.

- A major assumption at the conference was that birth rates needed to be reduced in less developed countries.

- Less developed countries were blamed for:

  - Global overpopulation.

  - Decline of natural resources.

  - Migration pressure on developed countries.

- The professor criticized this assumption because:

  - Most powerful leaders were from developed countries.

  - Few leaders from less developed countries had enough influence.

  - Less developed countries became **voiceless** in the debate.

- Western feminists saw the conference as an opportunity:

  - Their organizations were recognized in their own countries but not always abroad.

  - Working with world leaders could open doors into less developed countries.

  - Population control aligned partly with feminist goals of **reproductive autonomy**.

- Western feminists did not fully agree that Global South people caused overpopulation, but they supported women's access to:

  - **Contraception**.

  - Control over pregnancy.

  - The right to decide when to marry and how many children to have.

- The reasoning:

  - If women have self-autonomy and access to contraception, birth rates may decrease.

#### Western Benefits of Not Having Children

- Western feminists promoted the benefits of not having children or having fewer children.

- Benefits mentioned in the lecture:

  - More free time.

  - More energy.

  - More opportunity to pursue goals.

  - More time for training and self-improvement.

  - Lower expenses.

  - More savings.

  - More financial stability.

  - More chance to travel, explore, and gain knowledge.

  - More independence.

  - Fewer burdens and more freedom.

- The professor's point:

#### Why Birth Rates May Be High in Less Developed Countries

- Women in less developed countries may view children as:

  - **Assets**.

  - Future income.

  - Family labor.

  - Support in old age.

- In agricultural societies:

  - Families need manpower for production.

  - Technology may be too expensive.

  - Families may not have the knowledge or resources to use advanced technology.

  - More children can mean more workers.

- Children may also be sent to cities to work and send money home.

- Religion and culture can support high birth rates:

  - Some religious beliefs encourage having many children.

  - Motherhood may be seen as a moral duty.

  - Giving birth may be connected to being a "good woman" or "good mother."

- Weak government support also matters:

  - If the government does not provide reliable welfare, elderly support, healthcare, or social services, families depend on children.

  - The professor used Thailand's small elderly allowance as an example of how difficult it can be to survive on state support alone.

- Contraception may be seen as:

  - Too expensive.

  - A waste of money.

  - Unnecessary or culturally wrong.

- Therefore, Western messages like "do not have children" can sound unrealistic or insensitive.

#### Beijing Conference and Global Feminist Tensions

- The slide identifies this case study as the **1995 Fourth World Conference on Women in Beijing**.

- The conference was supposed to discuss urgent issues faced by women in less developed countries.

- According to the professor, Western feminists "hijacked" the conference by pushing their own priorities:

  - **Reproductive rights**.

  - **Sexual freedom**.

  - **Discrimination based on sexual orientation**.

- Women from less developed countries became angry and less trusting because:

  - They felt Western feminists were not truly working for them.

  - They felt Western feminists wanted to look smart, modern, or morally superior.

  - They felt Western feminists did not understand poverty, survival, government corruption, and social instability.

- The slide adds that many Global South women did support reproductive and sexual rights, but argued that **economic survival** and **global inequality** also needed more attention.

#### Family Planning Programs, Loans, Corruption, and Debt

- Western feminists and international actors persuaded leaders in less developed countries to establish **family planning programs**.

- The professor emphasized that many leaders accepted these programs for personal benefit, not because they sincerely wanted to protect women.

- Under these programs, countries borrowed money from institutions such as:

  - **World Bank**.

  - **IMF**.

- Problems described in the lecture:

  - Corrupt leaders and government officials took part of the loan money.

  - Only a smaller portion was left for actual family planning programs.

  - Clinics required long-term funding for staff, specialists, practitioners, equipment, and supplies.

  - Programs were supposed to provide free tools such as condoms and birth control pills.

  - Funding was not enough to run clinics properly.

  - Staff were not trained well.

  - Equipment ran out.

  - Clinics were abandoned.

- Results:

  - Birth rates stayed high.

  - Women's problems continued.

  - Countries became poorer.

  - Debt to the IMF and World Bank increased.

  - Dependence on developed countries increased.

- The professor used this to return to the exam debate:

  - Are women's problems in less developed countries caused mainly by **patriarchy**?

  - Or are they caused mainly by **underdevelopment**?
