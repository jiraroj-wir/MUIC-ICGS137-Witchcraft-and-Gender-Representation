## Notes

### Why People Practiced Witchcraft

- People used witchcraft because they wanted practical help with problems that felt urgent.

- One major reason was **healing**:

    - Disease could spread quickly and kill many people.

    - People wanted cures for symptoms they experienced in daily life.

    - Witchcraft offered herbs, rituals, charms, and other practices that seemed to give direct remedies.

- Another reason was **protection from evil spirits**:

    - People feared that unseen spirits could harm them.

    - Witchcraft gave them rituals, objects, or spells that seemed to defend them from danger.

- Witchcraft also promised **luck**:

    - People wanted to avoid bad luck or reverse a period of misfortune.

    - A witch could be approached to improve luck, protect a relationship, or create a better outcome.

- Witchcraft appealed because it offered **immediate emotional relief**:

    - Major religions often asked people to wait, endure suffering, or understand suffering as karma, a test, or preparation for happiness after death.

    - Witchcraft seemed to offer a solution in the present moment.

    - A person who felt betrayed, angry, anxious, or desperate could feel better because the ritual made them believe action had already been taken.

- This practical function helps explain why people could see witchcraft as a form of **religion** or spiritual support:

    - It gave guidance.

    - It gave hope.

    - It helped people solve everyday problems.

    - It connected human beings with spirits, nature, or other supernatural power.

### Christian Condemnation of Witchcraft

- Witchcraft was condemned as evil because it challenged Christian religious authority.

- The main accusations were:

    - Witches worshipped women or female divine power.

    - Witches challenged the idea of **one God** by recognizing many gods or spirits, including female gods.

    - Witches were accused of using **Satanic power**.

- Certain imagined witch abilities were treated as signs of evil:

    - **Flying**, because ordinary humans were not supposed to fly.

    - **Invisibility**, because disappearing suggested supernatural power.

    - **Killing through magic**, because a spell could be imagined as murder without physical contact.

    - **Shape-shifting**, because the human body was seen as given by God and not something to transform into an animal form.

- Condemning witchcraft served several social and religious purposes:

    - It helped maintain the number of church followers.

    - It protected church wealth and authority.

    - It demonized women's religious and healing practices.

    - It reduced women's influence in society.

- Religious authorities faced a contradiction:

    - If they openly confirmed witches were real, they also implied that magic and Satanic help were real.

    - That could make people ask why they should rely on the church if supernatural help could be found elsewhere.

- The result was a public campaign that made witches appear dangerous while preserving the church's image as merciful.

### Witch Hunts And The Logic Of Certain Death

- The early campaign against witches expanded over time:

    - A crusade against witches appeared in the early eleventh century.

    - **Pope Gregory IX** authorized killing witches in the thirteenth century.

    - **Pope Innocent VIII** confirmed belief in witches in the late fifteenth century.

    - Full-scale killing grew across the fifteenth, sixteenth, and seventeenth centuries.

- Large witch hunts first appeared in significant numbers in southern France and Switzerland during the fourteenth and fifteenth centuries.

- The peak years of witch hunts occurred in southwest Germany from 1561 to 1670.

- Once a person was accused, the trial process looked legal on the surface but was deeply unjust.

- The accused often had no lawyer and had to defend themselves.

- Before trial, accused people could be held in dungeons and tortured:

    - They could be starved or fed rotten food.

    - They could be physically injured with torture devices.

    - They could be mentally abused through rumors, curses, threats, and false stories about their families.

- The purpose of this pressure was to force confession.

- The accused faced a trap:

    - **Confession** meant execution or punishment.

    - **Denial** could be treated as lying, which also led to punishment.

    - Once accused, the person had almost no real way to survive.

### Accusation Methods

- Witch accusation methods were irrational, but they gave communities a way to turn fear, jealousy, conflict, or suspicion into public punishment.

- **Witch cake**:

    - A person who believed they were cursed could use urine to bake a cake and feed it to a dog.

    - If the dog ran toward someone nearby, that person could be accused of witchcraft.

    - This method easily targeted neighbors or familiar people.

- **Weighing against Bibles**:

    - A person could be weighed against a stack of Bibles.

    - If the weight did not match, the person could be called a witch.

    - The method was easy to manipulate because the number of Bibles could change.

- **Searching the body**:

    - Moles, birthmarks, scars, extra nipples, or other body differences could be treated as witch marks.

    - Women often had scars from domestic labor, cooking, cleaning, farming, or household injury.

    - Normal signs of work and aging could become evidence.

- **Talking to oneself or singing alone**:

    - Everyday self-talk could be treated as proof that a person was communicating with spirits.

- **Keeping many animals**:

    - Having cats, dogs, or other animals could appear suspicious.

    - Talking to animals could be interpreted as speaking an animal language through Satanic power.

- **Reciting the Lord's Prayer**:

    - A nervous mistake in public recitation could be treated as proof of witchcraft.

    - Public pressure made mistakes more likely.

- **Physical ordeals**:

    - Carrying hot stones could become a test.

    - Being thrown into water could become a test.

    - Floating could be read as guilt, while sinking could prove innocence only after the person was already in danger of drowning.

- **Whispering to elderly women**:

    - Older women with hearing difficulties could fail to respond.

    - That silence could be interpreted as not understanding human language.

- **Sarcasm, dreams, and marriage history**:

    - Sarcastic comments about witchcraft could be treated as suspicious.

    - Dreams about ancestors, animals, flying, or unusual actions could be taken as real entry into a witch realm.

    - Women who had been married many times could be seen as outside the traditional norm.

### Effects Of Accusation

- Accusation destroyed privacy.

- Once someone was named as a witch, rumors spread quickly and damaged reputation.

- People could enter the accused person's home to search for evidence.

- The accused could be publicly humiliated, cursed, isolated, and watched by the community.

- The whole community could participate in punishment:

    - Neighbors might stop speaking to the accused.

    - The accused could be abandoned or alienated.

    - Gossip and condemnation could create severe psychological pressure.

- Accusation worked as **social control** because it made people afraid to act outside accepted norms.

### Why Older Women Were Targeted

- The church image of the witch often matched stereotypes of elderly women:

    - Sagging skin.

    - Missing teeth.

    - Crooked body.

    - Dark clothing.

    - Thin face.

    - Unusual appearance.

- Older women were also targeted because they had knowledge and experience:

    - They understood herbs and medicine.

    - They understood nature and seasonal patterns.

    - They could give advice.

    - They could become respected community leaders.

- This made them threatening in a society that did not trust women with authority.

- Many older women lacked protection:

    - They might not have a husband, family, or powerful male relative to defend them.

    - Poverty, widowhood, age, and social isolation made accusation easier.

- This is important for **intersectionality**:

    - Gender alone did not explain accusation.

    - Age, class, marital status, reputation, and social protection also shaped vulnerability.

### Witchcraft Went Underground

- As persecution increased, witches practiced rituals in secret places.

- Practicing publicly became dangerous because discovery could lead to accusation and execution.

- Secrecy created a major social problem:

    - If witchcraft was believed to happen in secret, then no one needed direct evidence.

    - People could claim that a ritual happened even if nobody saw it.

    - A private conflict could become an accusation.

- This made witchcraft a **secret crime** or **impossible crime**:

    - It was imagined as a crime done behind closed doors.

    - Only the witch supposedly knew how the pact or ritual happened.

    - The lack of visible evidence did not weaken accusation; it made accusation easier.

### Roles In Witch Trials

- Witch-trial evidence was organized through four major roles.

- **Accusers**:

    - Accusers named the person they believed was a witch.

    - Accusations could come from fear, rumor, conflict, jealousy, or social tension.

- **Interrogators**:

    - Interrogators questioned the accused.

    - Questions often pushed the accused toward confession.

    - Leading questions could make denial difficult and confession seem like the only possible answer.

- **Watchers**:

    - Watchers observed behavior in the community.

    - They remembered or reported actions from the past, such as talking alone, speaking to animals, or behaving outside the norm.

    - In court, watchers could act like witnesses.

- **Searchers**:

    - Searchers entered the accused person's home.

    - They looked for unusual objects that could be presented as evidence.

    - Ordinary objects could become suspicious when interpreted through fear.

- These roles created a power imbalance:

    - Many people could produce accusations or evidence against one person.

    - The accused had little protection.

    - Community participation made the trial feel legitimate even when the evidence was weak.

### Witch Hunters And Malleus Maleficarum

- **Malleus Maleficarum** means **The Hammer of Witches**.

- It was written by two German Dominicans in 1486.

- The book became a major guide for identifying, hunting, interrogating, and punishing witches.

- It labeled witchcraft as **heresy**.

- For more than a century, it sold extremely widely in Europe and helped spread witch-hunting ideas.

- The book shows how print culture helped turn local fear into a broader system of persecution.

### Witchcraft In The Elizabethan Age

- The Elizabethan age was connected to the Renaissance and the expansion of print culture.

- The printing press spread religious and spiritual ideas, including ideas about witches.

- **Queen Elizabeth I** passed the **1562 Elizabethan Witchcraft Act**, formally titled **Agaynst Conjuracions Inchauntmentes and Witchecraftes**.

- Under the English witchcraft laws of 1562, the punishment for witches could be hanging.

- Queen Elizabeth's mother, **Anne Boleyn**, had been accused of witchcraft.

- Because witchcraft was imagined as something that could pass from mother to daughter, this created suspicion around Elizabeth herself.

- Elizabeth answered that suspicion by publicly condemning witches:

    - Condemning witches made her appear separate from witchcraft.

    - It turned attention away from the question of whether she had inherited witch power.

    - It also strengthened her authority by aligning her rule with anti-witchcraft law.

### Blaming Witches During Crisis

- Witches were blamed for major crises because people wanted a clear cause for suffering.

- During the **Black Death** or **bubonic plague**, people did not understand the actual causes of disease or how to cure it.

- Blaming witches gave fearful communities an explanation.

- A more accurate explanation is that bubonic plague spread through disease conditions, not witchcraft.

- Witches were also blamed for:

    - Bad harvests.

    - Failed crops.

    - Food shortage.

    - Weather changes.

    - Fires that destroyed houses.

- Blaming witches protected other people from responsibility:

    - A person responsible for a fire could avoid fines or punishment by blaming witchcraft.

    - A community facing crop failure could blame magic rather than environmental, economic, or human causes.

- This pattern shows **scapegoating**:

    - Fear created pressure for an explanation.

    - Authority named witches as the cause.

    - Punishing witches made the community feel safer even when the real problem remained unsolved.

### Gender, Literacy, And Class In Elizabethan Accusations

- Women were often denied education, which made them more vulnerable in legal and religious settings.

- Many people still relied on wise women for remedies because they had practical healing knowledge.

- At the same time, the meaning of witchcraft expanded so that more types of behavior and more types of people could be accused.

- Elizabethan society was class-based.

- Accusations often targeted people without protection, including single women, older women, poor women, and women who kept pets for company.

- The death figures discussed for Elizabethan society were **247 women** and **23 men**, showing that women were targeted far more often than men.

### Black Magic, White Magic, And Witch Doctors

- **Black magic** meant magic imagined as harmful or malicious.

- Examples of black magic included:

    - Killing spells.

    - Attacking someone through magic.

    - Forcing someone to love another person.

    - Curses and hexes.

    - True-name spells.

    - Immortality rituals.

    - Necromancy.

- **Voodoo** was often associated with black magic in popular imagination, especially through fiction and stereotypes.

- **White magic** meant natural or beneficial magic.

- White magic was associated with:

    - Self-healing.

    - Protection.

    - Blessings.

    - Charms.

    - Curing illness.

    - Divining the future.

    - Interpreting dreams.

    - Finding lost items.

    - Generating good luck and well-being.

    - Controlling weather for harvest.

    - Nature spirits, mother goddess imagery, goddess worship, and oneness with nature.

- **White witches** were often seen as wise women or cunning folk.

- A **witch doctor** was originally a type of healer who focused especially on curing people.

### Christian Claims About Witchcraft

- Christian critics claimed that witchcraft rejected Jesus and the sacraments.

- They also claimed witches formed pacts with the devil to gain powers.

- These powers were imagined as ways to solve emotionally intense problems:

    - Infertility.

    - Fear for children's well-being.

    - Revenge against a lover.

- These accusations turned ordinary fears, family problems, illness, and romantic suffering into evidence of evil.
