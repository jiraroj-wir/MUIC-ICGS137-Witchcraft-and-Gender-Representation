## Notes

### Tituba As Outsider

- **Tituba** was an enslaved woman from Barbados who was brought to the New World and lived in the household of **Reverend Samuel Parris**.

- She did domestic work such as cooking and cleaning.

- Puritan society treated her as an outsider because she did not look, speak, worship, or behave like the white Puritan community around her.

- Her difference was read as danger:

    - She was a woman.

    - She was enslaved.

    - She was racially and culturally different.

    - She had no legal power to defend herself.

    - Even the household that owned her could not provide real protection against accusation.

- Tituba kept parts of her own culture after arriving in the New World.

- She told stories, sang songs, played music, and referred to spirits or deities that Puritans did not recognize.

- Puritans interpreted unfamiliar cultural practices as suspicious because their worldview divided daily life into **God's work** and **the Devil's work**.

- Cultural difference became evidence of moral danger.

### Healing, Spirits, And Demonization

- Tituba also had knowledge of plants, herbs, and healing.

- People accepted her help when treatments worked, but they became suspicious when healing was combined with blessings, spirit language, or ancestor protection.

- Her belief that ancestor spirits followed and protected her became threatening in a Puritan context.

- This shows a larger process of **demonization**:

    - Folk healing and spiritual practices were not automatically harmful.

    - Puritan fear and Christian demonology could redefine unfamiliar spirituality as evil.

    - Non-Christian language about spirits could be treated as evidence of contact with the Devil.

- This matters because witchcraft accusations often depended less on actual harm and more on how authorities interpreted difference.

### Tituba's Confession And Intersectionality

- Tituba was pressured and abused until she confessed.

- Her confession included claims that she had met the Devil, seen other witches, and signed an evil book.

- The confession did not simply end her case; it helped spread fear because it suggested that more witches were hidden in the community.

- Tituba was jailed rather than executed.

- Her later sale from jail shows how enslaved people could be treated as property even after public punishment.

- Tituba's case is an **intersectional** case because several forms of oppression overlapped:

    - **Race** made her visibly different from the Puritan majority.

    - **Enslavement** denied her legal rights and protection.

    - **Gender** made her vulnerable in a patriarchal society.

    - **Religion and culture** made her practices easy to label as evil.

    - **Class and status** left her without social power.

- Her accusation was not random; it followed the structure of the society around her.

### Sarah Good: Poverty And Anger As Targets

- **Sarah Good** was poor, homeless, and dependent on public charity.

- Puritan society often treated poverty as a **moral failing**, especially for women.

- Her appearance and living condition made her socially vulnerable:

    - She was thin and ragged.

    - She lacked a stable household.

    - She did not match the image of a respectable Puritan woman.

- Sarah Good was also known for anger, cursing, and challenging authority.

- In Puritan gender norms, a "good" woman was expected to be obedient, quiet, modest, and controlled.

- Sarah Good's anger was interpreted as a sign of moral disorder.

- Her poverty created a contradiction:

    - She was judged for not managing a proper household.

    - She did not have a house to manage in the first place.

- She was also judged as a failed mother because she could not provide shelter and stability for her child.

- Her case shows how **class**, **gender**, and **motherhood expectations** could combine to make a woman suspicious.

### Sarah Good's Daughter

- Sarah Good refused to confess.

- Authorities used her daughter as a point of pressure.

- The child was made vulnerable because children could be easier to frighten, isolate, or force into confession.

- When the daughter confessed to being a witch, the confession was used against Sarah Good too.

- This reflects the belief that witchcraft could pass through family lines.

- The case shows that even children were not safe from the logic of accusation.

### Martha Corey: Speech And Independence

- **Martha Corey** was a church member and a respected woman.

- She was intelligent, outspoken, and able to speak about religious matters.

- She criticized the witchcraft accusations and questioned how quickly the community accepted them.

- Her criticism made her dangerous to the authorities because she challenged the logic of the trials.

- Martha Corey was targeted because she:

    - Spoke her mind.

    - Questioned accusations.

    - Challenged Puritan leaders.

    - Refused to submit when society turned against her.

    - Represented female independence in a society that expected obedience.

- Her case shows how accusation could silence women who criticized public power.

### Bridget Bishop: Economic Independence And Reputation

- **Bridget Bishop** was an independent business owner.

- Her economic independence already placed her outside the expected role of women.

- Puritan gender norms did not encourage women to own property, run businesses, or act publicly independent.

- Bridget Bishop's behavior was also treated as nonconforming:

    - She wore bright colors instead of modest clothing.

    - She decorated herself with flowers.

    - She worked in a tavern-like social space.

    - She talked and joked with men.

    - She had a public reputation shaped by gossip.

- Gossip mattered because Puritan communities used surveillance and rumor to police behavior.

- Women who did not fit the ideal image of modest femininity could be turned into public threats.

- Bridget Bishop's case shows how accusations punished women who were economically independent, socially visible, and sexually suspected.

### The Spark Of The Salem Witch Trials

- The first major panic began in **1692** in the Parris household.

- Two young girls, **Betty Parris** and **Abigail Williams**, began showing strange behavior:

    - Fits.

    - Screaming.

    - Convulsions.

    - Claims of being pinched, burned, or attacked.

    - Breathing difficulty or physical distress.

- The community could not easily explain the symptoms.

- The local doctor, **William Griggs**, could not identify an ordinary medical cause.

- Instead of saying the cause was unknown, the symptoms were interpreted as the work of witchcraft.

- Once the behavior was named as a witch's curse, the community began searching for someone to blame.

### Pressure To Name Witches

- Adults pressured the girls to identify who had harmed them.

- The girls had a reason to continue the story because admitting that the behavior was performance or attention-seeking could bring punishment.

- Under pressure, they named women who were already vulnerable or disliked:

    - **Tituba**, the enslaved outsider.

    - **Sarah Good**, the poor homeless woman.

    - **Sarah Osborne**, an older woman who rarely attended church.

- Sarah Osborne's absence from church was treated suspiciously because church attendance was central to Puritan moral life.

- The first accusations show a pattern:

    - The accused were not the most powerful people in the community.

    - They were women already marked as outsiders.

    - Existing prejudice made the accusations believable.

### How Fear Escalated

- After the first accusations, fear spread quickly.

- Tituba's confession that other witches existed intensified the panic.

- Fear affected both the accused and the wider community.

- Effects on the accused:

    - Fear of execution.

    - Pressure to confess to things they had not done.

    - Isolation and loss of control over their own defense.

- Effects on the community:

    - Paranoia.

    - Hallucinations or imagined signs.

    - Suspicion toward neighbors and family members.

    - Dream interpretation treated as possible evidence.

    - Loss of trust inside the community.

- This is the key idea of **mass hysteria**:

    - Fear spreads through a group.

    - People become irrational and illogical.

    - Concern for fairness decreases.

    - Rumor begins to replace evidence.

### Hatred, Property, And Social Control

- Accusations were not only about religion.

- They could also be shaped by hatred, envy, property conflict, and personal revenge.

- Accusing someone of witchcraft could remove that person from the community.

- Once someone was accused, their home and property became vulnerable.

- Taking property from an accused witch could be socially excused because the accused person was treated as evil.

- Witch hunts also targeted outspoken people.

- When outspoken people were silenced, society lost criticism and debate.

- Without criticism, communities became less able to correct mistakes or improve.

- Witchcraft accusations therefore worked as **social control**:

    - They punished deviation.

    - They protected existing authority.

    - They discouraged women from speaking publicly.

    - They made conformity feel necessary for survival.

### Puritan Beliefs And Religious Background

- Puritan society was shaped by strict religious beliefs and little tolerance for disagreement.

- Everyday life was interpreted through a religious frame:

    - Actions could be seen as godly or evil.

    - The Devil was believed to be active in daily life.

    - Unexplainable behavior could be interpreted as Satan's work.

- Women were often viewed as morally weaker, more emotional, and easier for the Devil to corrupt.

- Obedience to religious authority was treated as necessary for community survival.

- This created a culture of:

    - Fear.

    - Suspicion.

    - Discipline.

    - Gossip.

    - Surveillance.

    - Control over women's behavior.

### Why The Hunt Increased

- The desire to end the witch hunt quickly made the hunt larger.

- People believed that if all witches were found and punished immediately, the community could return to safety.

- This logic created more accusations:

    - Fear increased pressure to identify witches.

    - Pressure increased confessions.

    - Confessions reinforced belief in hidden witches.

    - Belief in hidden witches justified more hunting.

- The result was a cycle of fear and accusation.

### End Of The Trials

- By early **1693**, authorities recognized that many accusations were false.

- The governor stopped the trials.

- The colony later apologized.

- The damage could not be undone.

- The trials resulted in:

    - More than **200 people accused** of witchcraft.

    - **19 people executed**, mostly women.

    - **5 people dying in jail**.

    - **1 man pressed to death**.

- There was no adequate punishment for the authorities and accusers who had produced false justice.

### Causes Of The Trials

- Several forces caused the Salem Witch Trials:

    - **Intense religious fear**: unexplainable events were interpreted as Satan's work.

    - **Strict social control**: Puritan society wanted to purify itself by removing people outside the norm.

    - **Community tension and conflict**: envy, hatred, gossip, and property disputes shaped accusations.

    - **Patriarchal authority**: men maintained power through strict rules and religious discipline.

    - **Fear, hysteria, and pressure to confess**: panic made people want to identify and punish witches quickly.

### Why The Salem Witch Trials Matter

- The trials exposed the danger of **mass hysteria**.

- Fear can lead to **human rights violations**.

- Fear can blind people to truth and replace reason with panic.

- Fear can turn neighbors into enemies.

- The trials revealed flaws in the legal system:

    - Accused people lacked lawyers.

    - Evidence was weak.

    - Rumor and dreams could be treated as meaningful proof.

    - Confessions could be forced through pressure.

    - People could be imprisoned or executed without fair protection.

- The trials created an early conversation about **individual rights**:

    - The right to defend oneself.

    - The right to legal protection.

    - The right to keep property.

    - The right not to be humiliated or exposed in public searches.

- The trials show how society punishes people who do not fit the norm.

### Gender-Based Violence And Social Control

- The Salem Witch Trials represent **gender-based violence** because women were targeted through systems that punished female nonconformity.

- Violence appeared in several forms:

    - Execution.

    - Imprisonment.

    - Public humiliation.

    - Forced confession.

    - Silencing.

    - Loss of property.

    - Racial and class bias.

- Accusations reinforced male authority by threatening women who:

    - Challenged the church.

    - Spoke too much.

    - Owned property or ran businesses.

    - Acted confidently.

    - Resisted expected obedience.

    - Showed sexual freedom or public sociability.

- Religious language hid violence by framing punishment as:

    - Protecting the community.

    - Purifying the town.

    - Defending God against the Devil.
